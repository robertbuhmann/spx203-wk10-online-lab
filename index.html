Lab overview

This online lab asks you to apply the full exercise prescription process in a practical and evidence-informed way. You will analyse two gym-based sessions, justify the selected exercises and prescription variables, and then propose appropriate alternatives that preserve the intended adaptation.

The two cases are:

  • a sprint athlete aiming to improve lower-limb pushing power for faster starts
  • a generally healthy older adult aiming to improve lower-limb power for activities of daily living

Learning objectives

  • Explain the intended adaptation of a gym-based exercise session.
  • Justify exercise selection, sets, repetitions, intensity, and rest using scientific literature.
  • Identify alternative resistance or plyometric exercises that preserve the intended adaptation.
  • Support substitutions using scientific evidence where possible.
  • Use first principles when direct evidence is limited.
Important disclaimer

You may not always find a paper that directly supports the exact exercise, set and rep scheme, or substitution you choose. Strong answers use the best relevant evidence available and then explain the applied reasoning. Consider specificity, overload, progression, movement pattern, muscle groups targeted, contraction type, force-velocity characteristics, and the functional demands of the client.

What counts as evidence?

  • Systematic reviews and meta-analyses
  • Randomised controlled trials
  • Biomechanical studies of muscle forces or movement demands
  • Training studies in similar populations
  • Guidelines from reputable professional bodies

When evidence is limited

Use first principles. Explain how your decision is supported by movement pattern, muscle groups, force and velocity demands, contraction type, stability demands, unilateral/bilateral demands, functional transfer, and the client’s goal.

Example of stronger reasoning

Weak: “I chose a goblet squat because it is easier.”

Stronger: “I chose a goblet squat as a regression for the older adult because it preserves the sit-to-stand/squat movement pattern, targets the hip and knee extensors, and reduces technical and loading demands compared with a heavier bilateral squat. I would adjust the load so the concentric phase can still be performed quickly and safely.”
